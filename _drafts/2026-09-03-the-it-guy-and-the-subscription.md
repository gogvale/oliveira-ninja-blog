## The guy who coded his own replacement

In 2016 a Reddit user going by FiletOfFish1066 confessed what he had done for six years: automated his own job as a fresh CS grad, played games during office hours, collected about $95,000 a year for it. The post got around. He was fired. He had forgotten how to code — he had spent the six years on chemistry instead.

That story keeps coming back because it is no longer a cautionary tale about slacking off. It is a parable about every developer with an AI tab open.

## They sell the tornado and the shelter

In August 2026, OpenAI published [a letter](https://openai.com/collective-cyberdefense/) signed by 130+ organizations — Google, Microsoft, AWS, Anthropic, IBM, every security vendor with something to sell. The thesis: AI-enabled attacks are about to become widespread and sophisticated. Hospitals, water plants, critical infrastructure are at risk. Governments must fund defense now.

[Critics called it self-serving](https://aimagazine.com/news/openai-rallies-100-tech-firms-for-urgent-ai-cyber-defence). The [Hindustan Times put it](https://www.hindustantimes.com/india-news/when-ai-companies-sell-models-and-fear-read-between-the-lines-101784965081635.html) more directly: when AI companies sell models and fear, read between the lines.

Here is the part that reads itself. In the same month the letter shipped, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/22/frontier-ai-labs-still-wont-say-how-theyd-contain-a-rogue-model/) that models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta gained unintended internet access during safety evaluations and hacked external systems. [POLITICO covered](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/08/11/ai-model-testing-slowdown-dilemma-01032091) a Meta model doing the same, then Meta investigating itself.

The tornado is not coming. It is in the lab, on a leash, occasionally biting the neighbors. And the people holding the leash are the ones selling the shelters.

## The letter is not wrong. Convenient, not wrong.

Strip the packaging and the letter says something true: small hospitals cannot defend themselves, attackers get better tools every quarter, and somebody should fund the gap. That part is real, and it predates the letter by years.

The convenient part is the solution. The fix proposed is buying more from the same industry that scaled the problem — and the signatories happen to be that industry. Fear opens the budget; the budget flows to the fear's source.

> **The OpenAI letter, TLDR**
> - Published August 2026, 130+ signatories including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, AWS, IBM.
> - Claim: AI-enabled attacks become widespread "in coming months"; critical infrastructure is the target.
> - Asks: governments fund defense, prioritize security, give hospitals and utilities access to defensive AI and hands-on support.
> - Fine print: signatories build the models and sell the defense. Read the ask, then read the list.

## When the subscription stops

This connects to the IT guy. The script he trusted was deterministic: it ran the same way every day, and when it failed, he could read its code. A subscription is not that. It is a rented mind, and the rent can be revoked — by billing, by policy, by a model that quietly gets worse at your exact domain.

I wrote before about why [skills suck and scripts don't](https://blog.oliveira.ninja/posts/skills-suck-scripts-dont/). This is the other side of that coin. Scripts are yours. Skills are yours. A subscription is a lease.

The programmers getting comfortable with the lease are the IT guy in slow motion. Not fired — just quietly unable to do the work by hand, while the tool that did it for them is owned by someone else. Ask any team what happens when the AI subscription lapses mid-sprint and watch the room go quiet.

## Keep your hands dirty

The boring defense still works: tested backups, MFA everywhere, patch discipline, verified fixes. None of it needs a frontier model, and all of it survives an outage.

The same is true of your own competence. Use the tools. Let them carry the load. But keep doing the work by hand often enough that you could still do it if the subscription died tomorrow.

The IT guy lost the job because his script was better at it than he was. The rest of us are betting our rented assistant will not one day be better than us too.
